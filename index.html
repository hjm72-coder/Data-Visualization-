# Club Sports Are Cursed by Pay to Play *How the commercialization of youth sports decides who gets to dream — and who gets priced out.* The price tag starts before a child can even tie their own cleats. In affluent suburbs, families are paying as much as $3,500 in club fees for nine-year-olds, according to reporting from [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/07/12/sport/youth-soccer-costs-united-states). A father in Tennessee, who asked not to be named for fear his sons' clubs would treat them differently, estimates he spends $15,000 a year on soccer for his two boys. His reasoning is blunt: "Money makes a difference in how good your kid is." Over the last two decades, youth sports in America have quietly morphed from a community pastime into a $40 billion industry, an economy nearly twice the size of the NFL's annual revenue, according to the [Aspen Institute's Project Play](https://projectplay.org/news/2025/2/24/project-play-survey-family-spending-on-youth-sports-rises-46-over-five-years). And as the money has flowed in, the doorway to athletic opportunity has narrowed to those who can afford the bill. The average U.S. sports family spent $1,016 on a single child's primary sport in 2024 — a 46% increase since 2019, and more than double the rate of general U.S. price inflation over the same period, according to [Project Play](https://projectplay.org/news/2025/2/24/project-play-survey-family-spending-on-youth-sports-rises-46-over-five-years). Across all of a child's sports activities, families paid nearly $1,500 annually per child. At the extremes, parents reported spending anywhere from nothing to almost $25,000. The increases cut across the most popular sports: spending on basketball jumped 105% over five years, soccer 69%, and baseball 68%. Soccer, where the club structure commonly includes paid coaches and tournament travel starting at the U-10 level, had the highest primary-sport spending among the major youth sports tracked. --- [![Average Annual Cost by Sport: 2019 vs. 2024](images/average-cost-by-sport.png)](https://www.datawrapper.de/_/NY1mG/?v=2) *Click the graph above to view the interactive Datawrapper visualization.* --- But the averages mask a deeper divide. The wealthiest families spent $1,471 more per year on a child's primary sport than families earning under $50,000, [Project Play](https://projectplay.org/news/2025/2/24/project-play-survey-family-spending-on-youth-sports-rises-46-over-five-years) found. Middle-class households were closer in spending to the lowest-income families than to the wealthy. Children from lower-income households were three times less likely to play on traveling teams than their wealthier peers, according to a Project Play survey cited by [The Modesto Bee](https://www.modbee.com/news/local/article316367368.html). > "Youth sports inflation is out of control and no segment of the population is untouched," said Tom Farrey, executive director of the Aspen Institute's Sports & Society Program. "Parents will spend just about anything for their children but when more money is being wrung out of fewer families, we're leaving a lot of opportunity on the table — denying many kids the benefits of sports. It's time to get serious about systems-level solutions that can get and keep more kids in the game." **Additional quote on the affordability gap?** The biggest single line item is no longer registration or equipment, it's travel. Travel and lodging averaged roughly $280 per sport, per child, in 2024, making it the largest spending category in the [Project Play](https://projectplay.org/news/2025/2/24/project-play-survey-family-spending-on-youth-sports-rises-46-over-five-years) survey. Private lessons and instruction added another $165 per sport per child. Together, travel and private coaching account for nearly half of household sports spending. --- [![Cost Breakdown: Where the Money Goes](images/cost-breakdown.png)](https://www.datawrapper.de/_/EZOAv/) *Click the graph above to view the interactive Datawrapper visualization.* --- > "League costs are not what's really impacting most parents. It's the travel," said Skip Gilbert, former CEO of US Youth Soccer. "For a lot of parents, it's not just the team. Many will get into private coaching and they'll start to get into all of the add-ons that they feel they need." That structural shift from seasonal, school-based play, toward year-round travel and club programs is at the heart of the cost explosion. Kyle Helton, a coach at the elite travel level, sees the change up close everyday. > "When I was playing, we were told to kind of go out and play on our own," Helton said. "We were told to just get comfortable on the ball, whether it's juggling, kicking the ball off the wall, so many different things that you can do on your own. Where, I think, now everything is shifted so the only way that you can get good training is to go and pay somebody a lot of money to do it." **Additional quote on travel/privatization costs?** The quiet injustice at the center of the pay-to-play model: it doesn't simply reward talent. It rewards access. According to the recruiting service NCSA, roughly 90% of college-recruited athletes participated in club sports, a statistic cited in the [Wayland Student Press](https://waylandstudentpress.com/128729/sports/the-cost-of-club-sports-affecting-athletes-both-financially-and-emotionally/) that "suggests that participation in club sports directly correlates to an athlete's success in recruitment." The implication cuts both ways: if the path to college runs through the club system, and the club system costs thousands, then the path to college is, in practice, a paywall. "It's simply a supply and demand deal, as more kids are joining, the more the fee is," the student publication noted. Club sports are "elevated" teams "separate from school related teams" that "tend to offer more playing, practice time, opportunities and exposure," with rigor "often equal to the private schools of the school systems." For Heather Maddocks, a freshman playing for New England Futbol Club (NEFC), the bill is around $5,000. > "There's also bags, uniforms, the whole NEFC kit, and they don't cover plane tickets or hotel fees," she said. The result is a system where a mediocre player whose family can write the check fills a roster spot that a more talented kid — whose family cannot — never even gets to compete for. The financial burden carries an emotional tax. For 15-year-old Makenna Johnson, a soccer player on a travel team ranked third in Georgia and 22nd nationally, the stakes are inseparable from her family's finances. Her mother, Meliesha — a single mother and teacher — estimates she spends $10,000 to $12,000 a year on soccer, even with team assistance and help from "angels." > "We do not have money like these other people," Meliesha told [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/07/12/sport/youth-soccer-costs-united-states). "It affects us financially more than other people. We came too far. The stakes are much more for us. You cannot blow this opportunity." The pressure lands hardest on the kids. > "It just hurts sometimes because I feel like I'm not putting in enough," Makenna said. "Or it leads me to believe I'm not doing enough when I'm really trying to give it my all. Or it makes me question my ability and wonder if, like, I'm actually as good as I think I am." Her mother sees the weight of it. > "I know she does," Meliesha said of whether her daughter feels more pressure than the girls who don't need soccer quite so much. "She knows our house is different, she knows our car is different. It's a little upsetting for me, because it's tough. I feel like I'm fighting to make her feel like she isn't different." --- [![Youth Sports Cost Index vs. General Inflation](images/youth-sports-vs-inflation.png)](https://www.datawrapper.de/_/0lo17/) *Click the graph above to view the interactive Datawrapper visualization.* --- The American model stands in stark contrast to how the rest of the world identifies and develops soccer talent. > "When you look globally, there are going to be kids that are playing in the streets that are not prohibited from playing every day barefoot, playing futsal, playing street soccer, and they're able to do that and be identified in ways that it's kind of hard to be identified in the states if you're not doing some of these events," Helton said. **Add context here - more about US vs other countries spending on club sports** > "If you want to be considered, to be seen by some of the best, you've got to put yourself in that environment," said Jeffrey Golston, who coached Makenna at TopHat. "As a family, you sit there and have to decide, OK, is this a goal of mine? And as a parent, if it's a goal of your kid, I feel that you've got to do what you've got to do to make your kid's dream pretty much come true." His conclusion: > "You have to, that's a non-negotiable."
